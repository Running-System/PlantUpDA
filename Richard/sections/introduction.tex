\section{Introduction}

This chapter examines how real-time plant monitoring can be implemented in a microservices-based IoT system under the practical constraints of consumer hardware. It introduces the application context of ``Plant Up!'', outlines the engineering conditions that shape protocol selection, and defines the research question that motivates the comparison between MQTT and REST.

\subsection{Background and Context}

Over the past decade, the Internet of Things (IoT) has moved from being a conceptual vision to a technology embedded in everyday life. Connected devices now monitor and influence physical environments in ways that would have seemed impractical only a few years ago. This development is particularly visible in agriculture. The concept of ``Agriculture 4.0'' captures the transition from experience-based decision-making toward data-driven optimization supported by sensor networks and automated systems \cite{leherAg40}.

Within industrial contexts, these technologies have already led to measurable efficiency gains. Precision irrigation, automated fertilization, and large-scale environmental monitoring are no longer experimental ideas; they have become established practices. Around the same time, a comparable transformation has begun to emerge on a much smaller scale in urban environments. Under the label of Smart Urban Gardening, digital tools are increasingly integrated to support plant cultivation in apartments, on balconies, and within community gardens \cite{streetsolverSmartGarden}.

From a broader perspective, this trend is closely connected to ongoing societal developments. Rapid urbanization, reduced living space, and a growing awareness of sustainability have all contributed to renewed interest in self-cultivation and local food production \cite{growdirectorUrbanAg}. In parallel, the concept of the Social Internet of Things (SIoT) has emerged. This extends the traditional IoT model by allowing objects to form relationships not only with other devices but also with users \cite{bpbSiot}. Under such a framework, connected objects become part of a social ecosystem rather than functioning merely as isolated technical components \cite{jungWeon2025}.

When applied to plant care, this perspective implies that a plant, if supported by suitable sensors and network connectivity, can communicate its condition digitally. It is therefore no longer perceived solely as a passive object requiring observation, but rather as an entity whose needs can be represented and shared through data. Although this idea may appear conceptual at first, it has clear engineering consequences, particularly when it is implemented using affordable consumer-grade hardware.

Unlike industrial systems that operate with stable power supplies and professionally managed infrastructure, consumer IoT devices must function under much tighter constraints \cite{kaaiotChallenges}. Devices placed in residential homes often rely on limited battery power and must operate within congested and sometimes unstable Wi-Fi networks. Under these circumstances, hardware selection, firmware design, and the choice of communication protocols must all be considered carefully \cite{emnifyChallenges}. Even small inefficiencies that might be negligible in an industrial setting can become critical flaws in battery-powered deployments.

\subsection{Case Study: The ``Plant Up!'' Project}

The present thesis investigates some of these challenges through the case study of the ``Plant Up!'' project. The system was conceived as an IoT-based application combining continuous plant monitoring with gamification elements in order to make routine plant care more engaging \cite{smarticoGamification}. Rather than simply displaying raw sensor values, the platform translates biological processes into more meaningful digital feedback for the user.

Its architecture follows a three-tier model consisting of distributed sensor nodes, a scalable cloud-based backend, and a mobile application interface \cite{cheThreeTier}. At the edge layer, ESP32-based devices measure environmental parameters such as soil moisture, ambient temperature, humidity, and light intensity. These measurements are then transmitted to the cloud infrastructure, where microservices process the incoming data, apply business logic, and update the persistent system state. The mobile application subsequently retrieves and visualizes the information in a format intended to emphasize progress, interactive achievements, and overall plant well-being.

While this overall structure may appear straightforward, the implementation reveals several architectural tensions. The continuous interaction between constrained edge devices and an expanding cloud backend requires careful coordination. Decisions made at the protocol level can directly influence system responsiveness, energy consumption, and the users' perceived reliability of the platform.

\subsection{Problem Statement}

Designing a socially integrated, near real-time plant monitoring system exposes a fundamental trade-off between latency, energy efficiency, and data consistency. On one side of the equation, gamification mechanisms rely on immediate feedback. When a user physically waters a plant, the expectation is that this action is reflected in the application almost instantly, without noticeable delay \cite{spinifyGamification}. Delayed responses pose a significant risk of reducing user engagement over time.

Nevertheless, battery-powered sensor nodes must minimize their energy consumption if they are to remain practical for everyday home use. The ESP32 platform, for instance, provides a Deep Sleep mode that significantly reduces current draw by disabling both the CPU and the Wi-Fi subsystem \cite{progElecDeepSleep}. Waking from this state, however, requires full reinitialization and network reconnection, a process that introduces both latency and additional energy expenditure \cite{redditEsp32Wake}.

This recurrent process leads to what can be described as a ``wake-up tax'': before any useful data can be transmitted, the device must re-establish its network connectivity \cite{redditEsp32Wake}. The communication protocol chosen for telemetry transmission directly affects the magnitude of this unavoidable overhead. Traditional HTTP-based REST communication is widely supported and conceptually simple, yet it involves relatively verbose textual headers and heavier message formats. MQTT, in contrast, was designed specifically for constrained devices. It uses a lightweight binary protocol structure organized around a publish/subscribe model \cite{psiborg2024mqtt}.

At this point, it is necessary to consider not only immediate performance metrics but also overall system consistency. In distributed systems, the CAP theorem states that consistency, availability, and partition tolerance cannot be fully guaranteed at the same time in the presence of network failures \cite{designGurusCAP}. In residential IoT environments, temporary network partitions are not exceptional events; they are common occurrences. The architecture must therefore tolerate intermittent connectivity while maintaining an acceptable level of data correctness and service continuity.

Building upon these considerations, the central research question of this thesis is formulated as follows:

\textit{How do MQTT and REST compare in a microservices-based architecture for real-time plant monitoring, specifically with respect to latency, throughput, and data consistency, when constrained by the energy limitations of battery-powered IoT devices?}

To approach this question methodically, the following chapters first outline the most relevant theoretical foundations regarding modern communication protocols and distributed systems. The specific system architecture of the ``Plant Up!'' project is then examined in more detail, highlighting the design decisions that guided its development. Finally, these architectural decisions are evaluated through a set of controlled experimental measurements in order to assess their practical implications.
