\section{Plant Up! System Architecture and Implementation}

\subsection{System Overview and Vision}
The ``Plant Up!'' platform integrates distributed IoT hardware, a scalable cloud microservices backend, and a mobile application into a single, unified ecosystem. This represents the practical realization of the Social Internet of Things (SIoT) concept discussed in the theoretical background: enabling plants to communicate their environmental status digitally to their caretakers. The primary objective is to collect high-precision environmental data from custom edge devices, synchronize it through the cloud, and present it to users through gamified interactions.

To realize this vision, the architecture must address three critical requirements:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Low-latency sensor synchronization: When a plant requires attention, the user must be notified promptly. The loop between biological need and human action must be closed as rapidly as possible.
    \item Energy-efficient operation: IoT devices intended for home use must be designed for long-term deployment, conserving battery power through intelligent deep sleep cycles and minimal radio usage.
    \item Distributed consistency: With data distributed across User, Social, and Hardware domains, the system must maintain a coherent state even during periods of network instability.
\end{itemize}

These requirements informed every architectural decision, from the specific sensors integrated into the hardware to the structure of the microservices. The resulting design follows a multi-layered approach: the Edge Node, the Cloud Layer, and the Application Layer.

\subsection{Hardware Layer: The Edge Node}
The hardware layer constitutes the physical sensing infrastructure of the project. It serves as the bridge between the digital cloud and the physical plant environment. The design centers on the ESP32-S3 microcontroller, equipped with a comprehensive suite of sensors for temperature, humidity, light, soil moisture, and electrical conductivity. This configuration provides a complete picture of the plant's health. Due to battery constraints, the device spends the majority of its operational time in a deep sleep state. Its operational cycle follows a strict sequence:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Acquisition: The device wakes, powers the sensors, and captures an environmental snapshot.
    \item Serialization: The sensor readings are packed into a compact JSON format.
    \item Transmission: The data packet is transmitted to the cloud (via MQTT or REST), after which the device returns to deep sleep immediately.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Component Selection and Sensor Interface}
Hardware selection involves a careful trade-off between capability and energy longevity. The following components were selected for the ``Plant Up!'' edge node.

Microcontroller: Espressif ESP32-S3 ---
The ESP32-S3 serves as the central processing unit. It was selected for its balance of computational power and energy efficiency. It features a dual-core processor and built-in Wi-Fi and Bluetooth connectivity. Of particular relevance is its Ultra-Low Power (ULP) co-processor, which allows the main CPU to remain in a sleep state while basic monitoring continues in the background. At approximately 20 Euro, it offers a favorable cost-to-performance ratio.

Soil Moisture Sensor: HiLetgo LM393 ---
The HiLetgo LM393 (approximately 7.89 Euro) measures the dielectric permittivity of the soil. Unlike lower-cost sensors that are prone to corrosion, this resistive sensor provides reliable moisture readings essential for triggering watering notifications.

Light Sensor: HiLetgo BH1750 ---
The BH1750 (approximately 11.39 Euro) is a digital I2C sensor that provides precise lux readings. This enables the system to assess whether a plant is receiving adequate light exposure and to generate corresponding notifications.

Electrical Conductivity (EC) Sensor: DFRobot Gravity V2 ---
The DFRobot Gravity Analog EC Sensor (approximately 12.10 Euro) measures the electrical conductivity of the soil, which correlates directly with nutrient concentration.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Relevance of EC:} Electrical conductivity correlates directly with nutrient levels. A plant may be adequately watered yet deficient in nitrogen. This sensor enables ``Plant Up!'' to predict fertilization needs, elevating the system from a simple watering alarm to a comprehensive health monitor.
\end{itemize}

Power Supply and Sustainability ---
To enable remote, long-term operation, the device pairs a lithium-ion battery with a 0.5\,W photovoltaic solar panel (approximately 3.48 Euro). This combination is designed to be self-sustaining, with a USB-C port included as a fallback charging option.

\subsection{Microservice Architecture Design}
The backend is structured as a collection of microservices organized around Supabase schemas. Each schema functions as a Bounded Context \cite{fowlerMicroservices}, encapsulating a specific domain:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{user\_schema}: Manages user profiles, streaks, and virtual wallets.
    \item \texttt{social\_media\_schema}: Manages community content: posts, comments, and plant profiles.
    \item \texttt{microcontroller\_schema}: Serves as the data warehouse for all sensor readings.
    \item \texttt{gamification}: Contains the game engine: quests, progress tracking, and XP.
\end{itemize}

This separation provides significant development and operational benefits. Modifications to the quest system, for example, can be made without risking disruptions to the sensor ingestion pipeline. Failures in one domain do not cascade to other domains.

For instance, the \texttt{Plants} table in the social schema links a user's plant to its ideal growing conditions stored in \texttt{Plant\_data}. This allows other services to retrieve botanical reference data without introducing dependencies into the social database.

\subsection{Real-Time Data Synchronization Mechanism}
A central challenge in the ``Plant Up!'' architecture is balancing real-time data delivery with energy conservation on the sensor devices. Users expect to see fresh data promptly, yet the sensor nodes cannot afford the energy cost of maintaining persistent connections. This subsection explains the reasoning behind the adopted synchronization strategy.

Two primary contenders were evaluated for the sensor-to-cloud communication:
\begin{itemize}
    \item REST (HTTPS): The standard web approach. It is structured and widely supported, making it well-suited for user-facing API interactions. However, for transmitting small sensor packets at regular intervals, the connection overhead is substantial.
    \item MQTT: A protocol designed specifically for IoT scenarios. It is lightweight and uses a publish/subscribe model, which is theoretically well-matched to the constraints of battery-powered devices such as the ESP32.
\end{itemize}

A hybrid approach was adopted to leverage the strengths of both protocols. The rationale for this decision is as follows: MQTT is used exclusively for the sensor-to-cloud communication path because its lightweight binary protocol and persistent broker connection minimize the energy expenditure per transmission cycle. However, the user-facing mobile application communicates with the backend through standard REST APIs, as this path does not face the same energy constraints and benefits from the request-response semantics of HTTP for loading user profiles and social content.

In this architecture, the IoT devices publish sensor data via MQTT to a broker. A dedicated backend service subscribes to the relevant topics and persists the incoming data to the database. The mobile application then retrieves this data through RESTful endpoints.

\subsection{User Engagement and Data Processing}
The social and gamification components of ``Plant Up!'' are not merely cosmetic additions; they serve as the primary mechanism for driving user engagement. This logic resides in the \texttt{gamification} schema and monitors the data streaming in from the other layers.

It relies on three core tables:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Quests: The catalog of challenges, such as ``Water your Monstera'' or ``Check the light level''.
    \item User\_quests: The individual user's progress on assigned quests.
    \item player\_stats: The user's cumulative statistics: XP and levels.
\end{itemize}
